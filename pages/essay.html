Intelligence for Everyone: The Case for Universal, Essentially Free AI

Every few generations, humanity invents something so fundamental that access to it stops being a luxury and starts being a right. Fire. The printing press. Electricity. The public library. The internet. Each of these technologies began as the privilege of a few and ended as the inheritance of all — and each time, the societies that universalized access fastest reaped the greatest rewards.

Artificial intelligence is the next entry on that list. In fact, it may be the most important one, because AI is not merely a tool — it is a tool for using tools, a lever that amplifies every other human capability. The question before us is not whether AI will reshape civilization; it already is. The question is whether that reshaping will lift everyone, or only those who can afford the subscription.

The answer should be obvious: AI must be universal and essentially free. Here is why.

1. AI Is Becoming the Interface to All Human Knowledge

For most of history, access to knowledge was rationed by geography and wealth. The printing press broke the first barrier; the internet broke the second. But the internet gave us access to documents — it still required the skill, time, and education to find, interpret, and apply information.

AI closes that final gap. It doesn’t just retrieve knowledge; it explains it, translates it, tailors it to your level, and applies it to your problem. A farmer can ask about crop disease in her own language. A teenager can have a physics concept explained five different ways until it clicks. A small-business owner can get a contract reviewed before signing it.

When a technology becomes the primary interface between people and the sum of human knowledge, putting it behind a paywall is functionally identical to putting knowledge itself behind a paywall. We decided centuries ago that was unacceptable. It’s why public libraries exist.

2. The Equity Argument: Preventing the Intelligence Divide

The defining inequality of the twentieth century was the digital divide — those with internet access pulled ahead, those without fell behind. The defining inequality of the twenty-first threatens to be the intelligence divide: those with AI assistance will think faster, learn faster, build faster, and earn more, while those without it compete with one hand tied behind their backs.

This divide compounds. AI-assisted students get better grades and better jobs. AI-assisted workers are more productive and more promotable. AI-assisted entrepreneurs outmaneuver those without it. Left to market forces alone, cognitive leverage becomes another asset the wealthy accumulate and the poor lack — except this asset makes its owner better at acquiring every other asset.

Universal free access is the only intervention that stops this flywheel before it starts. We don’t charge children by the hour for public school teachers. We shouldn’t charge them for the tutors that live in their pockets either.

3. Education: A Tutor for Every Mind on Earth

Benjamin Bloom’s famous “two sigma problem” showed that one-on-one tutoring moves the average student to the 98th percentile — but personal tutors were always too expensive to scale. AI is the first technology in history that makes personalized tutoring nearly free to deliver.

Every child on Earth could have a patient, infinitely knowledgeable tutor that adapts to their pace, their language, their learning style. Every adult could retrain for a new career without tuition. Every curious mind could pursue any subject to any depth without gatekeepers. Gating this behind paywalls doesn’t just hurt individuals — it shrinks the total pool of educated, capable people, which impoverishes everyone.

4. Health: Medical Guidance Where There Are No Doctors

Half the world lacks access to essential health services. AI is not a replacement for physicians, but it is a vastly better alternative to nothing — and for billions of people, nothing is the current option. Symptom triage, medication guidance, chronic disease management, mental health first aid, translation of medical jargon into plain language: these save lives today, and their marginal cost approaches zero.

Charging people for basic AI health guidance means rationing health information by income — a moral position few would defend out loud.

5. The Economic Argument: A Rising Floor Lifts Everyone

Universal AI is not charity; it’s macroeconomics. When every small business has access to the same marketing, legal, accounting, and analytical capabilities as a large corporation, competition intensifies and innovation accelerates. When every worker is more productive, wages and output rise. When every citizen can navigate bureaucracy, start a company, or learn a skill, the entire economy grows.

The twentieth century proved this pattern with public education and rural electrification: massive public investments in universal capability paid for themselves many times over through growth. Free AI is the same bet, with a much lower price tag — because unlike roads and power lines, the marginal cost of serving one more AI user is nearly nothing.

6. The Knowledge Commons: AI Was Built by All of Us

There is a moral argument here that deserves to be stated plainly. AI models are trained on the collective output of humanity: our books, our code, our conversations, our art, our scientific papers, our forum posts, our Wikipedia edits. No company invented this raw material. It is the accumulated intellectual commons of our species.

When the value extracted from humanity’s shared knowledge is sold back to humanity at whatever price the market will bear, something has gone wrong. This doesn’t mean AI companies shouldn’t profit — the engineering, compute, and research are genuinely expensive and genuinely valuable. But it does mean there is a powerful ethical claim that a baseline of AI capability, derived from everyone’s contributions, should be available to everyone.

7. Accessibility: For Many, AI Is Not a Convenience but a Prosthesis

For people who are blind, AI that describes the world is sight-adjacent. For people who are deaf, real-time transcription is hearing-adjacent. For people with dyslexia, ADHD, autism, or motor impairments, AI is a cognitive and communicative prosthesis that can be life-changing.

We would consider it cruel to charge a subscription for a wheelchair ramp. As AI becomes essential to navigating the world, charging for it erects a paywall in front of people with disabilities — the people who often benefit most and can least afford to pay.

8. Democracy and Civic Life

Free societies depend on informed citizens who can understand legislation, evaluate claims, access legal rights, and participate in public life. AI dramatically lowers the cost of all of this: summarizing a bill, checking a politician’s claim against the record, understanding your rights as a tenant or employee, drafting a complaint to a regulator.

If these capabilities are reserved for paying customers, then civic competence itself becomes a class privilege. Universal AI is civic infrastructure — as important to a functioning democracy in this century as a free press was in the last.

9. The Safety Case for Universality

Counterintuitively, universal access may be safer than restricted access. Concentrated AI capability means concentrated power: a handful of corporations and governments deciding what billions may know and do. Broad access distributes that power, enables independent scrutiny, lets researchers and journalists everywhere audit and challenge these systems, and ensures AI’s values are shaped by all of humanity rather than a few zip codes.

A world where only the wealthy and powerful have strong AI is not a safer world. It is a more controllable one — which is precisely the problem.

10. We Have Done This Before

Skeptics say free AI is unrealistic. History disagrees. We built public libraries when books were expensive. We built free public schools when education was a luxury. We built the web to be free at the point of use. Wikipedia — an audacious, “unrealistic” project — became the greatest repository of knowledge ever assembled, free to all. Linux and open-source software quietly run most of the world’s servers and phones.

Again and again, humanity has decided that foundational capabilities belong to everyone. AI is the next decision point.

Answering the Objections

“Compute is expensive. Someone has to pay.” True — but compute costs are falling by orders of magnitude, and we already know how to fund universal services: public investment, tiered pricing (free baseline, paid premium), open-source models, and treating core AI capacity as public infrastructure like roads and power grids. “Expensive to build” has never meant “impossible to make free to use” — see highways, libraries, and GPS.

“Free kills innovation.” The evidence says otherwise. The free, open internet produced more innovation than any walled garden ever did. Open-source software underpins trillion-dollar industries. Free baseline access expands the market, the talent pool, and the demand for premium products.

“Universal access means universal misuse.” Misuse is a real risk, but it is addressed with technical safeguards, norms, and law — not with price discrimination. Keeping AI expensive doesn’t keep it out of the hands of determined bad actors; it keeps it out of the hands of the poor.

What “Essentially Free” Actually Means

The goal is not that every AI product costs nothing. It is that a strong baseline of AI capability — tutoring, translation, writing, analysis, coding help, health and legal information — is available to every person on Earth at zero or near-zero cost, the way search engines and email are today. Premium tiers for heavy users and enterprises can and should exist above that floor. What matters is that no one is locked out of the floor.

Conclusion: The Inheritance of Our Species

Artificial intelligence is distilled human knowledge, trained on everything we have ever written down, now capable of giving it back as understanding, skill, and capability. The choice in front of us is whether that inheritance is sold to the highest bidder or shared like the commons it came from.

We have stood at this crossroads before — with books, with schooling, with electricity, with the internet — and every time we chose universality, civilization leapt forward and the “unrealistic idealists” were proven to be the realists. AI is the most powerful lever on human potential ever created. A lever that only the rich can pull will pry inequality open wider than anything in history. A lever that everyone can pull could lift the species.

Make it universal. Make it free. It already belongs to all of us — it’s time the access did too.
