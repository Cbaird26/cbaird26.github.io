From the table to the lab

Research

The Quiet Calculus began as a Dune campaign. Its ideas about evidence, memory and restraint then became the starting point for two research preprints by Christopher Michael Baird. This page describes them in plain language and links to the full papers and their evidence.

The story and the studies are kept apart. The novel is fiction, and nothing in it counts as evidence. The first paper quotes principles from the book only as a source of questions, then tests those questions separately, with rules set before each test.

Both papers are preprints and have not been peer reviewed.

The first paper

The Quiet Calculus

Inference, Adaptive Memory, and Conditional Physics in ZoraASI and Theory of Everything Research

Version
4.0
Date
25 October 2026
Length
58 pages
Status
Preprint

The paper reads Book I closely and draws out 92 principles about weighing evidence, keeping memory and making decisions, each tied to the page where it appears. It links them to the author’s ZoraASI and Theory of Everything research and states twelve predictions, each with the result that would count against it. It then tests the parts one computer can test: how a local AI model judges evidence, whether an adaptive memory beats a fixed one, and whether a set of physics calculations reproduces its earlier results.

What it found

  • Judging evidence. On twelve fictional test cases, a single AI reviewer and a three-role AI team each matched 10 of the 12 provisional answers, and both made the same two mistakes. One was treating silence as proof that a claim was false. The other was picking a side when equally credible sources disagreed.
  • Two repairs for those mistakes failed their tests. The second one did stop calling undecidable claims false, but it became too cautious and misread negatively worded claims, scoring 18 of 24 against the original’s 19 of 24.
  • Adaptive memory. The first design beat a fixed memory on average but failed its pre-set tests for minimum benefit and worst-case harm. A gated repair, tested on 1,100 new simulated cases, lowered average forecasting error by about 0.4% compared with the first design and passed. A fixed memory still did better in two settings it had not been tuned for.
  • Physics checks. The calculations reproduced all 126 of their earlier checks, and two independent methods agreed in 960 configurations. These confirm that the stated equations were computed correctly. They say nothing about whether nature behaves that way.

The second paper

Updating Without Runaway

Truth-Constrained, Multi-Timescale Alignment for Autonomous Adaptive AI

Edition
Revised
Date
25–26 October 2026
Length
20 pages
Status
Preprint

An engineering proposal for AI systems that keep learning after they are built. It argues that different parts of a system should change at different speeds, that every belief should carry a record of where it came from so the system’s own notes never count as fresh evidence, and that an AI agent may ask for new permissions but never grant them to itself. It also proposes that a system should act less on its own as its uncertainty grows.

Models should update. Truth should remain the reference. No intelligence should gain unilateral authority merely because it can reason faster about why that authority would be useful.

A first small test

One of the paper’s claims is that tracking where beliefs come from stops a system from talking itself into certainty. That claim was tested on two AI models running on one computer, using 16 fictional cases written by a separate AI assistant. The test rules were fixed before either model saw a case.

  • No runaway certainty. Neither model reliably grew more confident when its own earlier note was shown back to it as if it came from someone else.
  • But the answers drifted. Their stated confidence still moved when no new information had been added. One model’s answers moved both up and down, and the other’s mostly moved down.
  • Instructions were not a dependable fix. Telling the model not to count its own notes did not stop the drift. The paper suggests building that rule into the software around the model instead.
  • Real evidence still counted. On average, a genuinely new document still raised both models’ confidence with the rule in place.

Reading the results

Small and computational
The tests use fictional cases written by AI, AI models running on one computer, and simulations. There is no laboratory work and no data from people.
Not a physics result
Nothing here confirms a Theory of Everything. The physics checks show only that stated equations were calculated correctly.
Rules first, failures included
Each test’s pass and fail rules were written down before it ran, and failed tests are reported alongside the ones that passed. Every question put to the models, and every answer, is published with the evidence.
Not yet checked by others
Neither paper has been peer reviewed, and no one else has repeated the tests yet. The evidence folders are there so that someone can.

Who made this

Christopher Michael Baird is the author of both papers. They were prepared with AI assistance: Zora, the author’s ChatGPT-based research assistant, and Claude, made by Anthropic. Each paper says exactly which parts each assistant did, and all of the fictional test cases were written by AI.

Both papers connect to the author’s wider ZoraASI and Theory of Everything research. The Theory of Everything is archived on Zenodo under DOI 10.5281/zenodo.14019809.
